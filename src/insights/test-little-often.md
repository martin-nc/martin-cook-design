Imagine you ran a fruit stall in a market. Every day you can see which fruit people look at, and which they buy. They ask you questions like, "Have you got any strawberries?" and you can say "Yes, they're over there at the bottom". Then they can tell you, "Oh, I didn't see them there! I was looking higher up." So you know you have to make your strawberries more visible.

Or you can ask them questions like, "Did you fancy any grapes today?" And they might say, "No, I don't like those grapes. They're full of pips." So you know to change the grapes you sell. You are continuously discovering what works on your stall, you are building relationships and learning why people visit. Perhaps one person always makes a fruit salad on a Thursday, or another goes on a weekend detox every month, and eats only apples for a couple of days. You hear the context behind each visit.

Now imagine you make it big, and you run a national chain of fruit shops. You operate from a swish office at your headquarters. How do you decide now what works for your shops? You probably have stats and analytics for sales and visits, but not the stories behind them. You've lost that contact with your customers, and the insights you get from it. 

This our position with websites. Ideally, we'd be like the stall holder, building relationships, observing and chatting, but instead we're in our office guessing. User testing takes you back down to the market stall, to see how people actually use your site, and away from your guesswork and from the solumn counsel of self-appointed experts.

## Why get feedback

- **You need to verify you are on the right track.** The longer we work in a domain or organisation, the more we become a frog in a well. We assume the language we use is intuitive, and the knowledge we have is widespread. This is one way we suffer [false consensus](/insights/beware-false-consensus/). We need to verify that the language and the structure of the site is intuitive, and that its interface is as easy to use as we think it is.
- **Users have brilliant ideas.** If you can't think what to call a group of pages, or you can't decide where to put some content on the site, then don't be afraid to ask your users. They often come up with fabulous ideas about how to organise and design your site.
- **The key to commitment is involvement.** If you ask for people's feedback on a site, you are showing them that you value their opinion. This makes them more interested in the site, and more likely to give feedback in future. If you get feedback within an organisation, then talk to the people who normally don't get asked their opinion, like the people lower down the hierarchy in the admin or reception. These people are often less immersed in jargon than the management, and they are usually customer or user-facing, so they know what people ask and complain about.

## Feedback is iterative

If you test with two or three people early on, it will be clear if people can't see a button or can't find a menu item. You can fix this and then in your next tests your users will get past those issues and find other issues. 

If you left the testing until just before the launch, and got thirty people in a room to test the site, most of them would be stuck on not finding that button or menu item, and you wouldn't get chance to fix the problems further on in the user journey.

## Case study: putting users over gurus

In 2013 I was redesiging the King's College, Cambridge, website. I read a blog article by a big name in web development, who said "If you work on a university website, then you need to use the label 'Admissions' for information about applying to the university. This is the standard term now." We already used "Admissions" in the site menu, so I thought, "That's fine".

Then I did a card sorting exercise where I made cards for each page or group of pages on the site. There were cards for things like "News", "Library" and "Undergraduate Study". Then I asked people to group the cards into categories, and give each category a name.

One person was stuck on a name for the admissions informations, so I said, "Would 'Admissions' work for you?"

"No, that wouldn't work at all," she said. "That would mean admissions to the chapel."

I was stunned: of course it could mean that! We'd never thought of that. This shows the huge value of feedback. It only takes one person to point out something you didn't think of, but when it's mentioned it's obvious. 

It also shows that it's better to base your decisions on what your users find intuitive, rather than the declarations of people who've never met them.

## Who to ask for feedback

Ideally, ask the audience you identified in your [strategy](/insights/dont-skip-strategy/), but even a friend or a colleague not involved in the project is better than no-one. Anyone will notice that you can't find a menu, even the non-target audience.

The worst people to ask are the ones familiar the project, and the ones steeped in the organisation's jargon (typically senior managers).

If you get feedback from someone with strong opinions, and who states authoritatively that "you need to do this" and "people won't like that", then be sure to balance their feedback with someone less opinionated. The most opinionated people aren't necessarily the most representative (see [Beware false consensus](/insights/beware-false-consensus/)).

## Case study: the CAF bank relaunch fiasco

<img src="/images/caf-bank.jpg" alt="A screenshot of the CAF bank home page, with a huge photograph of two people giving a high five" width="300" />
<figcaption>CAF Bank demonstrating Non-Functional Design</figcaption>
</figure>

In the UK there is a bank called the Charities Aid Foundation (CAF) Bank. It is used by charities to deposit their money and make payments. In June 2025, it launched a new website.

Within a week, they had so many complaints they had to close their phone lines, and then take down the website. Although many problems concerned the back end system (e.g. payments not going through), the front-end website also had issues. Examples include:

- **Unfamiliar symbols.** Some people couldn't find the main menu because it was a three-line [hamburger menu](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hamburger-menu-icon-recognizability/) under the logo, not at the top of the screen. In addition, the three line were unequal length so it looked like a paragraph symbol in Microsoft Word.
- **Inconsistent terminology.** On some pages a transaction was called a "transfer" and on others the same transaction was called a "payment". What's worse, these are different things (a transfer is when you transfer money between your own accounts, and a payment is when you pay an external party).
- **Inconsistent dialogues:** in some screens the positive options like "Yes" and "Continue" (versus "No" and "Go back") were on the left side of the dialogue box, and in other screens they were on the right. This led to people clicking the wrong options.
- **Context changes:** many people work for more than one charity. The new site allowed you to log in and choose the charity you wanted to deal with. Let's say you worked with charity X and charity Y. You could choose charity Y and arrive at screens showing the accounts for charity Y. But whilst working there you would suddenly find yourself in a charity X screen. As a result, it was easy to pay a bill for charity Y from charity X's accounts.
- **No system feedback:** if you tried to submit a form without filling all the mandatory fields then nothing happened. There was no error message to help.

Clearly no-one had watched a customer use this site before it was launched. Anyone interested in what customers said about it can look at their [comments on TrustPilot](https://uk.trustpilot.com/review/www.cafonline.org) (91% of reviews give it one star). Nearly a year later, the problems continue. What particulary annoyed some customers was that the bank refused to accept anything was seriously wrong (false consensus again).

## How to get feedback

### Informal chats

Feedback is an ongoing process, even after the launch of your new site or feature. Get all the feedback you can. Ask people at meetings and conferences, and in chats with colleagues, how they find the website. Did they find what they wanted? Is it easy to use?

### Informal test sessions

Every now and then, ask someone if they will test your site for you. This is especially important when developing a new site or feature. Tell them you want to watch them do a task, and give you some feedback on how easy it was. Emphasise that it won't be a test of them, but of the website.

I say this because some people say, "Oh, it's no use asking me about websites. I don't know anything about them." They have the impression you want to test them, not the website. Tell them that if they have problems on the site it's not their fault, it's the site design.

Sit by them, or if you're virtual then ask them to share their screen. Ask them what they have done on the site in the past, or what they did the last time they visited the site. Then ask them to show you what they did, and talk through their thoughts. It's important they keep talking and keep telling you what they expect to happen next, and what they think of the page they've just landed on. Was it what they expected? Is it clear what to do next?

If you are testing a particular feature like an application system, then of course you can make the task more focused, and ask people to go through that. Again, ask them to tell you what they're thinking, and don't give them any guidance. If they're stuck or confused, then ask, "What are you thinking?"

Be sure to take notes of the feedback. Compare the notes you take from different users and pick out the common themes. These tell you what you probably need to change.

### Formal sessions with a UX person

If your organisation has User Experience (UX) people then ask them to help you, or carry out some sessions. If not, and if you have the money, then you can hire UX consultants to conduct sessions for you, or just give you advice about how to conduct them. 

Be aware that if you want them to test some users, you will have to supply the names and contact details of volunteers. UX consultants don't usually find users for you, or have any on their books.

### Test without a design

If you are developing a new site, a site redesign or a new feature, don't wait until close to launch before testing. You can test even before the site is coded or has a design. For this, you can use "[wireframes](https://www.interaction-design.org/literature/topics/wireframe?srsltid=AfmBOoqmFu0QJrrGkM5XS1sA3x8wwT4HK059vfeOihzftyjckB6Wj-3O)". These are simple drawings of a web page (on mobile or desktop).

You draw a rectangle to represent the screen and then simple shapes to represent page elements. Horizontal lines represent text, boxes with a cross in them represent images and button shapes represent buttons. The important thing is to put in the main navigation links, so people can see where you can go and what you can do. This also allows you to test if the words you are using and the structure of the site is intuitive.

You can get software to make wireframes, but you don't need it. If you are face to face you can just draw them on paper, with the advantage that you can make and modify them in seconds. Instead of people clicking on links you can ask, "What would you click on?" and show the sheet with the drawing of the page they chose. You can also use PowerPoint or Google Slides and put links in to other slides, so the slides work like webpages, where you can journey from one wireframe to the next.

Many people use software like Figma to mock up wireframes or go straight to complete designs (with colours, photos and so on) for people to click through. But here it is easy to get distracted by aesthetics, when what underlies the site or app is the structure and language (the information architecture).

Wireframes remove distracting discussions about graphic design and let you focus on the essentials: how people will do what they want to do.

### A note on questionnaires

By "feedback" I don't mean online questionnaires. I mean talking to someone in real life and watching them use your site. When someone visits your site or app this is a small chapter in a story of their life. They were doing something before they arrived on your site and they're going to do something after.

Questionnaires are like web stats in that they don't tell you the entire context of why someone is on the site. They don't let you see how people use the site and how they feel about it, and its less likely you will get helpful suggestions from users, suggestions that arise as a result of just chatting about the site.


## Do you need to test your site?

This is where you have to be practical and honest. In some organisations, as long as you use the software the graphic design approach that's most fashionable, the stakeholders might not care if the site is usable. They might say there's no need because it looks fine to them. 

If this sounds cynical, here a little anecdote: I was once at a UX conference where we had lots of talks on user-centred design. Then we had a workshop where we were split into groups of four or five people a table. The facilitator gave each person a piece of paper with an archery target on it, with three concentric circles.

"Just by yourselves," she said, "in the centre of the target, I want you to write down the group of people who have most influence on your website design. Then in the second circle write the group who has the second most influence, and in the third write the group who has a little influence but not much." We did that.

Then she said, "Now show everyone on your table what you wrote." We put our sheets in the middle of the table and burst out laughing. Everyone had written "management" in the centre circle. The "users" were in the last circle. The management decided what went on the site, and weren't interested in what the users thought.
